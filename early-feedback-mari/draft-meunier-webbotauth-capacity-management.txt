                 PACE: Capability Management for Agents

Abstract

   This document addresses the noisy neighbour problem without requiring
   a stable client identifier at the origin.  Separately allocated
   budgets let origins limit consumption beneath a shared platform
   identity.  It describes how to manage budget continuity across
   requests using [MOLE-ARCH].

   The deployment described here combines Client budgets with shared
   infrastructure attribution for aggregate limits.  Composition with
   authorization primitives needs further work.

1.  Introduction

   Agents are increasingly used in business and user workflows,
   including AI assistants, search indexing, content aggregation, and
   automated testing.  Their relationship with origins is symbiotic.
   They need information from origins, and origins need signals to apply
   rate-limit policies.  These can be binary, like allow or block when
   an origin cannot serve traffic, or resource-based to allocate
   capacity among clients.

   Historically, most of these signals have been enshrined into
   deployments of internet protocols.  An agent needs an IP address to
   receive a response from an origin.  Its TLS stack and browser
   behavior provide further signals.  It provides a familiar User-Agent
   because middleboxes might otherwise filter its traffic (see
   [USER-AGENT-HISTORY]).

   Recent developments break these patterns.  Agents use proxies
   ([CONNECT-UDP], [CONNECT-IP], [OHTTP], or plain [HTTP]).  As
   platforms make requests on behalf of users, the user's TLS stack may
   no longer be the one visible to the origin.  AI agents, on the user's
   device or hosted remotely, fetch and render information in place of
   browsers.  User-Agent strings are still spoofed.

   An address, including an [IPv6] address, is not necessarily a user.
   Blocking a shared IP range can affect everyone behind it.  A platform
   signing requests under one [WBA-HTTPSIG] identity creates a similar
   shared fate: when one tenant's agent overloads an origin, limiting
   the platform also limits its other tenants.  [SIGNED-AGENT-METADATA]
   describes this problem and proposes signed product, project, and
   session identifiers to let an origin isolate traffic below the
   platform level.

   More granular identifiers are useful when the origin needs to
   recognize a particular product or project, investigate abuse with its
   platform, or follow a task within a session.  [SIGNED-AGENT-METADATA]
   advocates for such identifiers.  While they are pseudonymous, the
   receiving origin can link requests within the scope and lifetime of
   those identifiers.  This has several privacy costs:

   *  Discriminatory treatment: Explicit identifiers allow for
      discriminatory treatment of traffic based on those identifiers.
      An origin can give preferential treatment to some products or
      projects, or block them regardless of their requests' behavior.

   *  Linkability: Explicit identifiers allow for linking traffic to
      that identifier.  The scope and lifetime of the identifier,
      together with other request data, determine how much activity the
      origin can associate with it.

   *  Relationship disclosure: Explicit identifiers reveal information
      about the relationship between an intermediary and its clients,
      such as which requests belong to the same project.  Clients may
      have no clear view of how that information will be processed,
      stored, or shared.

   This presents a natural tension between the needs of an origin and
   the needs of a user.  Identifiers give an origin a way to apply
   capacity and abuse controls at the granularity provided.  This
   depends on the platform assigning them reliably.  Agents using those
   controls bear the cost of attribution.  An origin has to be able to
   apply policies to traffic whether it finds it abusive or acceptable.
   Bounding consumption does not always require more fine-grained
   identifiers.

   This document describes capacity budgets for agents using MOLE, an
   architecture for private moderation [MOLE-ARCH].  An Anchor allocates
   budgets to tenants, workloads, agents, or user accounts.  MOLE
   Clients obtain and spend those budgets (Section 2.1).  Separately
   allocated budgets let the origin charge one allocation without
   consuming another.  Anchor issuance and Moderator spending policies
   together constrain consumption at the chosen scope.  For example,
   Section 6 limits each tenant to twenty budgeted operation attempts
   per epoch without revealing which tenant made a request.  MOLE itself
   does not define tenant rate limits; the profile defined in this
   document provides them through these policies.

   Avoiding unnecessary disclosure supports the end-user interests
   discussed in [INTERNET-FOR-END-USER].  For these capacity decisions,
   the origin need not receive product, project, or session identifiers.
   When used, a platform signature remains visible, along with public
   spending terms and other request data.  This approach does not
   prevent discrimination based on that information.

   Such a mechanism is useful in a number of cases.  Some examples
   follow:

   1.  An AI agent service that hosts remote agents and concentrates
       their traffic on a small set of IP addresses.  One user's
       activity can affect others when an origin can only limit the
       shared address or the platform as a whole.

   2.  Network operators carry traffic from device-resident agents
       behind shared IP ranges.  Policies applied to an address range or
       an entire ASN give these users a shared fate.

   3.  VPN providers and enterprise zero trust gateways have a noisy
       neighbour problem.  They may know their users, but should not
       have to expose them to each origin.  An origin able to tell
       employees apart could single out an executive's activity for
       instance.

1.1.  Related work

   [SIGNED-AGENT-METADATA] addresses shared fate under a platform's Web
   Bot Auth signature with signed, pseudonymous sub-identifiers.  It
   lets an origin apply policy to a product, project, or session while
   the platform can map those identifiers back to its customers.
   Origin-scoped session values reduce cross-origin correlation
   (Section 7.2 of [SIGNED-AGENT-METADATA]).  However, each identifier
   still links requests within its own scope and lifetime.  As described
   in Section 6, this document does not specify tenant-specific abuse
   reporting.  An Anchor can constrain project allocations, but the
   origin cannot recognize and selectively block all of a project's
   independent Credentials from their presentations alone.

   [WBA-ABA] also uses MOLE for anonymous bot access and rate limiting.
   Its deployment relies on Anchor vetting and origin acceptance of
   issuance policies (Sections 3 and 4.1 of [WBA-ABA]).  It also
   discusses convergence on accepted policies (Section 4.3 of [WBA-ABA])
   and the risk of cheap registration yielding multiple credentials
   (Section 8.2 of [WBA-ABA]).  These are governance dependencies
   between Anchors and sites.  This approach shares those dependencies
   and focuses on allocations beneath a platform's identity, where the
   platform can use its existing tenant relationships to constrain new
   budgets.

   Poppy [POPPY] uses User IDs and sessions, including for anonymous
   browsing.  [AAP] uses anonymous admission tokens and recommends a
   session afterwards, though it also allows a fresh token per request.
   Here, the aim is to maintain a scarce, independently allocated budget
   without linking its presentations.

   [WIMSE-AIMS] profiles WIMSE and OAuth for agents within a defined
   trust domain.  It treats an agent as a workload with its own
   identifier and credential.  It carries the user, when there is one,
   only as the subject of an OAuth access token, so that the two
   identities are separate and compose on one request.  [WAG] lets a
   platform-hosted workload obtain such a token for itself without per-
   workload registration.  This document places both among the options
   an origin can ask for, next to attribution and anonymous capacity.

2.  Conventions and Definitions

   This document uses the HTTP terminology of [HTTP] and the MOLE
   terminology of [MOLE-ARCH].  The roles can be operated by separate
   parties or combined.

2.1.  Parties and hosting relationships

   *Platform:*  A service that hosts workloads for tenants.

   *Tenant:*  A customer organization, developer account, or billing
      workspace that runs workloads on a platform, often organized as a
      project.

   *Workload:*  A deployed application or service, as described in
      [WIMSE-ARCH].  A software product or service can comprise several
      workloads.

   *Agent:*  Software that performs tasks, potentially on behalf of a
      user.  A session is a bounded task execution or workflow run, not
      the agent itself.

   *User:*  A person on whose behalf an agent may act.

   A typical hosted deployment has this hierarchy:

   Platform
     Tenant
       Workload
         Agent

   A tenant runs workloads that execute agents.  Users can use several
   agents, and agents can serve several users or run unattended.
   Device-resident agents need no hosting tenant.

2.2.  MOLE roles and budget scopes

   *Client:*  The MOLE participant that obtains an Endorsement, redeems
      it for a Credential, and presents that Credential when requesting
      a resource.  An agent or a shared workload component can implement
      this protocol role.

   *Anchor:*  The party that grants Endorsements under a policy the
      Moderator accepts.  Its relationship with a Client constrains
      access to new budgets.

   *Moderator:*  The party that issues Credentials and verifies their
      presentations.  It sets the budget policy and may be operated by
      the origin or on its behalf.

   *Origin:*  The server that controls access to the requested resource.

   *Intermediary:*  A proxy, gateway, or tunnel on the request path.  An
      intermediary that terminates HTTP can sign forwarded requests
      under its own identity.

   A Client may hold several budgets, and an account or workload may
   receive budgets for several Clients.  Those allocations depend on
   Anchor and Moderator policy.  A Credential does not establish a
   unique account, workload, device, or other principal.

   MOLE(X) denotes a presentation for budget scope X.  MOLE(C) is a
   Client's budget, while MOLE(T), MOLE(W), and MOLE(U) denote tenant,
   workload, and account scopes.  These labels do not appear as
   identifiers in presentations.  WBA(Y) denotes a Web Bot Auth
   signature from intermediary Y.

2.3.  Endorsement examples

   An accepted Anchor could allocate Endorsements at each scope:

   *  Platform: A vetting service grants a hosting provider a finite
      pool.

   *  Tenant: The platform caps a retailer's workspace allocation under
      its subscription, as in Section 6.

   *  Workload: A deployment service authenticates the shopping-
      assistant workload and caps its allocation across replicas and
      restarts.

   *  Agent: The workload operator allocates a shopping agent's task
      budget against the workload's allowance.  New tasks do not reset
      that allowance.

   *  User: An account provider authenticates a customer and caps
      allocations across that account's agents.  This limits accounts,
      not unique people.

   Clients redeem these Endorsements for Credentials.  The Anchor
   accounts for the allocation scope, which presentations do not reveal.
   Capacity evidence does not grant permission.  Combining budget proofs
   remains undefined (Section 4.4).

3.  Scope and principles

   The aim is to bound consumption through independently allocated
   budgets without requiring a stable client identifier.  Exhausting one
   budget should not exhaust another allocation, provided Anchor
   issuance and Moderator spending policies keep them separate.  The
   privacy objective is to separate the relationship used to obtain a
   budget from the requests that spend it [PRIVACY-PARTITIONING].  A
   platform aggregate limit can still affect every Client behind it even
   when their individual budgets remain usable.

   For public resources, the deployment goal is a baseline level of
   access without a credential or signature.  This baseline remains
   subject to capacity and request behavior.  Scarce Endorsements can
   support access above it.  Absence of an active signal does not itself
   establish abuse [CIVIL].

   Permissioned resources may require evidence of a right before serving
   any content.  A capability can establish that right without
   identifying its holder.  Where a resource's policy depends on a
   particular principal and its permissions, authenticating the
   principal with a stable identifier is appropriate.  Capacity evidence
   alone establishes neither identity nor a right to perform a
   permissioned operation.

   Clients choose which evidence to offer.  Origins should ask only for
   evidence needed by the requested operation.  A verified identity does
   not establish that a request is harmless, and a valid budget does not
   grant every permission.  Moreover, client evidence should reveal only
   what the origin needs to apply its policy.  Network metadata and
   other request fields can still identify or correlate clients.

4.  Attribution and authorization

   An origin decides whether to serve an operation and how much capacity
   to allocate to it.  The evidence it accepts can establish
   attribution, permission, or capacity.  A request may need more than
   one.

4.1.  Attribution

   A verified WBA(P) signature attributes its covered components to a
   key published by P [WBA-HTTPSIG].  The origin can apply an aggregate
   limit to P without receiving an identifier for each Client behind it.
   The signature does not establish permission unless origin policy
   grants it to that signer.

   A workload can authenticate using a scheme such as [WIMSE-AIMS].  Its
   credential and signature profile differ from Web Bot Auth, even when
   both use HTTP Message Signatures [HTTPSIG].  A session identifier
   allows requests to be correlated within the session.  Either form of
   attribution can accompany a budget, with the resulting loss of
   unlinkability within its scope.

   An origin can request a signature with Accept-Signature.  The Client
   may decline, and the origin may refuse access where its policy
   requires a signature.  The public baseline in Section 3 is this
   deployment's goal, not a requirement imposed by Web Bot Auth.

4.2.  Permission

   Permission depends on the resource's policy.  An origin can accept a
   capability without identifying its holder, including a Privacy Pass
   token when the issuer's attestation satisfies that policy
   [PRIVACYPASS] [PRIVATETOKEN].  A reused capability can still link
   requests.

   An OAuth access token conveys an authorization grant [OAUTH].  The
   resource checks the token as its authorization server requires,
   including its audience, validity, permissions, and any proof of
   possession.  Identity obtained through [OIDC] or workload
   authentication can also be an input to access policy.  Authentication
   alone does not determine the permitted operations.

   A workload can obtain a token using [WAG], and an existing federation
   can support delegated access through [OAUTH-XAA].  These grants are
   exchanged at the authorization server, which decides what access
   token to issue.  They do not define a common token claim layout.
   [AAUTH] defines a separate signed authorization protocol with its own
   challenges and credentials.

4.3.  Capacity

   The budget flow uses the EC-ACT credential type of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS].
   Other MOLE credential types can have different properties.  An EC-ACT
   Credential holds a hidden balance c.  A presentation proves that it
   covers a requested spend s.  The moderator can return an amount t,
   bounded by s plus the offered top-up a.  The replacement credential
   holds c - s + t.  The origin learns the public amounts and that the
   spend was possible, without learning the balance or a stable client
   identifier [MOLE-PROTOCOLS].

   For example, a policy can issue B credits, charge one per accepted
   presentation, and return zero with no top-ups.  Each issuance then
   allows at most B fresh accepted operation attempts.  Further
   issuances increase that allocation.  Bounding consumption over time
   also requires policy for replenishment and expiry.  Refunding every
   spend in full leaves successful consumption unbounded.

4.4.  Composition

   A carrier signature and a MOLE presentation can travel on one
   request.  The signature should cover the presentation and the
   relevant request components, as discussed in Section 8.  Multiple
   signature profiles can use distinct labels, with each verifier
   applying the corresponding profile.

   An origin might require both an access token granting permission to
   use a resource and a MOLE presentation showing sufficient capacity.
   MOLE, PrivateToken, and OAuth bearer tokens [BEARER] use the
   Authorization field, which carries one credentials value
   Section 11.6.2 of [HTTP].  WWW-Authenticate can carry several
   challenges Section 11.6.1 of [HTTP], but does not express that all
   must be satisfied.

   The following illustrates the limitation; challenge parameters and
   credential values are omitted:

   Server response: two challenges
   WWW-Authenticate: Bearer ..., Mole ...

   Client request: one credentials value
   Authorization: Bearer ...

   The current MOLE transport also selects one presentation per request
   [MOLE-HTTP].  Carriage of several budgets or of a budget beside
   another authorization scheme, and signaling cumulative requirements,
   remain undefined here.

5.  Moderating consumption

   The origin's moderator configures its accepted Anchor Set, credential
   contexts, allocation and spending policy, and any permission
   requirements.  A client may send an initial request without evidence.
   The origin verifies what is present and serves the level of access
   its policy permits.  A public response can offer more capacity
   through a MOLE challenge.  A permissioned resource can refuse the
   operation and challenge for the missing authorization.

   A MOLE redemption challenge lets a client obtain a credential.  A
   presentation challenge specifies its spending terms.  Clients use the
   exchanges and verification rules of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS] and [MOLE-HTTP],
   including replay checks and request binding.  A failed proof supplies
   no evidence of budget.  A refund replaces the spent Credential with
   an updated one.  Returning zero credits leaves c - s credits.
   Recording NoUpdate ends the chain, as illustrated in Section 6.  The
   Moderator records this decision before attempting the operation,
   using the evidence available at that point.

   After a lost response, the Client can recover the recorded credential
   result within the retention period.  Recovery does not repeat the
   protected operation or establish whether it completed.  Application
   recovery remains separate.  One credential permits one presentation
   in flight at a time.

5.1.  Public access with WBA and MOLE

   The origin offers a budget above its public baseline.  Client C
   already holds an Endorsement from an accepted Anchor.  Intermediary P
   adds its own signature.  The Moderator is shown at the origin.

 +-------------+  +-------------+   +---------------+  +---------------+
 |   Anchor    |  |  Client C   |   | Intermediary  |  |    Origin     |
 |             |  |             |   |       P       |  |   Moderator   |
 +------+------+  +------+------+   +-------+-------+  +-------+-------+
        |  Endorsement   |                  |                  |
        +--------------->+                  |                  |
        |                |                  |                  |
        |                |     request      |                  |
        |                +----------------->+                  |
        |                |                  |                  |
        |                |                  |     + WBA(P)     |
        |                |                  +----------------->+
        |                |                  |                  |
        |                |      baseline + redemption and      |
        |                |       presentation challenges       |
        |                +<-----------------+------------------+
        |                |                  |                  |
        |                |           Redeem & Issue            |
        |                +------------------+----------------->+
        |                |                  |                  |
        |                |      Credential with balance c      |
        |                +<-----------------+------------------+
        |                |                  |                  |
        |                |request + MOLE(C) |                  |
        |                +----------------->+                  |
        |                |                  |                  |
        |                |                  |     + WBA(P)     |
        |                |                  +----------------->+
        |                |                  |                  |
        |                |   record spend, attempt operation   |
        |                |        response with Update         |
        |                +<-----------------+------------------+
        |                |                  |                  |

                    Client balance: c - s + t

   Figure 1: Public access with carrier attribution and an anonymous
                                 budget

   1.  The Client sends a request.  The intermediary adds WBA(P), which
       the origin verifies before applying its aggregate limit.

   2.  The origin serves the public baseline and offers redemption and
       presentation challenges on the 200 response.  A Client with a
       suitable Credential skips redemption.  Otherwise, it fetches the
       Moderator configuration and redeems its Endorsement at the Redeem
       & Issue endpoint.

   3.  The Client presents proof that its balance covers s and requests
       a top-up of at most a.  The Moderator verifies and records the
       spend result atomically before attempting the operation.

   4.  The response returns an Update with 0 <= t <= s + a, continuing
       the chain with balance c - s + t.  Each subsequent spend answers
       an applicable challenge and uses a fresh nullifier, without a
       credential link to the previous presentation.

   A direct Client uses the same exchange without WBA(P).  A Client
   unable or unwilling to present continues at the public baseline,
   subject to origin policy.  A failed presentation does not authorize
   the budgeted level of access.

6.  Budget limits and banning an abusive chain

   A platform hosts agents for two tenants.  Tenant A sends excessive
   requests while tenant B runs a shopping agent within its allocation.
   Both tenants' requests carry the same platform signature.  Blocking
   that signature would interrupt both tenants.  The origin needs to
   bound A's consumption and stop abusive requests without requiring
   either tenant to disclose a stable identifier.

   Suppose the platform also acts as an Anchor accepted by the origin.
   It grants at most two Endorsements per tenant per epoch.  Each
   Endorsement can be redeemed once at this Moderator for an EC-ACT
   Credential with ten credits.  The Moderator charges one credit per
   accepted operation attempt and returns an updated Credential with the
   remaining balance.  It returns no credits and offers no top-ups.
   Credentials expire at the epoch boundary.

   In this scenario, credits represent capacity granted by the Moderator
   under its own access policy.  Twenty credits correspond to the
   capacity allocated to a tenant per epoch.  A system based on
   fingerprints might instead apply a rate limit per IP address, User-
   Agent, or TLS fingerprint.

   Each tenant's allocation therefore permits at most twenty budgeted
   operation attempts in the epoch.  Starting another task does not
   reset the allowance.  This bound depends on the Anchor enforcing the
   allocation and on other accepted Anchors not supplying additional
   budgets.  The origin verifies each spend without learning which
   tenant's allocation it consumes.

   If the origin detects abuse on A's presented request, its Moderator
   can record NoUpdate and end that credential chain.  The chain is the
   sequence of Credentials obtained by updating one initial Credential.
   Its remaining credits are forfeited.  B's independently allocated
   Credentials remain usable.  Figure 2 shows this decision.  The tenant
   labels explain the example and are not identifiers carried in
   presentations.

   +-----------------------------------------------------------+
   |                 Platform acting as Anchor                 |
   |           At most two Endorsements per tenant             |
   +-------------+-------------------------------+-------------+
                 |                               |
                 v                               v
   +---------------------------+   +---------------------------+
   |         Tenant A          |   |         Tenant B          |
   |    Two 10-credit budgets  |   |    Two 10-credit budgets  |
   +-------------+-------------+   +-------------+-------------+
                 |                               |
          Abusive request                 Allowed request
          + presentation                  + presentation
                 |                               |
                 v                               v
   +-----------------------------------------------------------+
   |                   Origin and Moderator                    |
   |          Both requests carry the platform signature       |
   |          Each presentation spends a separate budget       |
   +-------------+-------------------------------+-------------+
                 |                               |
             NoUpdate                      Update (t=0)
                 |                               |
                 v                               v
   +---------------------------+   +---------------------------+
   |   Presented chain ends    |   |      Chain continues      |
   |   Remaining credits lost  |   |      One credit spent     |
   +---------------------------+   +---------------------------+

       Figure 2: Ending an abusive chain while an independent budget
                               remains usable

   This ban applies to the presented chain.  It does not revoke A's
   other Credential or prevent A from obtaining a later allocation.  A
   replacement must count against the tenant's allowance.  Exhaustion
   also prevents further spending from a budget, but an update returning
   zero credits does not end a chain that still has a sufficient
   balance.  Neither a task boundary nor a tenant boundary is
   established by the presentation itself.

   The platform's aggregate limit and the origin's available capacity
   still apply to both tenants.  Public-baseline requests are outside
   the illustrated budget.  Missing budget evidence does not itself
   establish abuse.

   For the origin to ask the platform to investigate a tenant or change
   its future allocations, they need a feedback arrangement.  One
   possible extension would require a fresh platform Endorsement before
   accepting an origin-issued Privacy Pass token, using a reverse-flow
   exchange.  Feedback could also occur out of band.  These are possible
   deployment arrangements, not exchanges specified here.  Their binding
   to the affected allocation, enforcement, and disclosure to each party
   remain to be defined.  They need not require a persistent tenant
   identifier at the origin.

7.  Deployment considerations

   Deployment separates two kinds of capacity control: an intermediary
   can identify itself for an aggregate limit, while an Anchor allocates
   scarce Endorsements for smaller, anonymous budgets.  Figure 3 shows
   the Anchor endorsing Client C while intermediary P carries its
   requests.  The same provider can operate both roles, but need not.  A
   tunnel carrying end-to-end TLS cannot add an HTTP signature.

   +----------+
   |  Anchor  |
   +----+-----+
        | Endorsement
        v
   +----------+           +--------------+           +-----------+
   | Client C |  MOLE(C)  | Intermediary |  MOLE(C)  |   Origin  |
   |          +---------->|      P       +---------->| Moderator |
   |          |           |              |  +WBA(P)  |           |
   +----------+           +--------------+           +-----------+

        Figure 3: Separate endorsement and forwarding relationships

   The origin can attribute the forwarded request to P through WBA(P)
   and charge C's separate budget through MOLE(C), without learning C's
   identity from that presentation.  The allocation relationship, rather
   than P's signature, establishes which smaller unit bears the spend.
   The following subsection applies this separation to product, project,
   and session capacity limits.

7.1.  Capacity without identifiers

   Signed product, project, and session IDs give an origin visible keys
   with which to keep separate counters [SIGNED-AGENT-METADATA].  For
   capacity alone, the Anchor can constrain Endorsements per product,
   project, or task allocation only if it knows and enforces those
   boundaries.  The platform can play this role.  An independent Anchor
   needs a way to account for the same allocations, or its Endorsements
   cannot enforce the platform's project and product totals.

   Clients redeem those endorsements for independent MOLE credentials
   and spend the credentials on requests.  The Moderator checks each
   spend and carries the remaining balance into an unlinkable
   replacement Credential.  The presentation itself does not reveal
   which product, project, task, or Client holds a particular budget.

   The platform can still sign WBA(P), so the origin can also limit its
   traffic in aggregate.  Credential contexts and spending terms should
   be shared across many endorsements.  Unique contexts or amounts would
   distinguish those units and shrink their anonymity sets.

   The allocation boundary can be chosen without adding a corresponding
   ID to requests:

   *  Product: A platform or other suitable Anchor can reserve a finite
      pool of Endorsements for a product and distribute them among its
      projects or Clients.  Spending is limited by the budgets derived
      from that pool.  The origin does not learn which product a
      presentation came from, nor can it impose a different per-product
      policy from presentations alone.

   *  Project: The platform can cap Endorsements for a tenant workspace
      and allocate them among its Clients.  Exhausting one Client's
      independent Credential does not exhaust another's.  The platform,
      not the origin, accounts for how many endorsements belong to that
      project.  A single project-wide balance shared by many concurrent
      Clients is a different requirement, discussed below.

   *  Session: An Anchor can allocate Endorsements to a task (like an
      ephemeral workload).  The Anchor must count task allocations
      against a scarce parent allowance so starting new tasks does not
      reset the limit.  The allocating party and Client must keep the
      resulting Credentials assigned to those tasks: a MOLE presentation
      does not prove which task is using a Credential.  Alternatively, a
      Client can spend the same Credential across tasks when task-
      specific budgets are unnecessary.  Neither choice lets the origin
      link requests as one task.  Such correlation requires an
      additional mechanism.

   The example in Section 6 bounds a tenant's total allocation while the
   origin checks each Credential separately.  Applying that policy to a
   project requires the Anchor to account for all allocations to that
   project.

   The Anchor can also cap the sum of Endorsements across projects
   belonging to one product.  The origin enforces each Credential's ten-
   credit limit without seeing the project or product.  The project and
   product totals depend on the Anchor's issuance policy, not on an
   origin-side counter keyed by an ID.  Additional accepted Anchors or
   Endorsements increase the available budget unless their policies
   constrain the combined allocation.  Public-baseline requests and the
   platform's aggregate limit remain separate from this bound.

   These budgets replace identifiers only for the capacity decisions
   their issuance and spending policies implement.  An origin cannot use
   an anonymous presentation to identify and block a particular project,
   audit its activity, or correlate all requests in a session.  If it
   needs those functions, signed metadata can accompany MOLE, making
   requests linkable at the metadata's scope.

7.2.  Capacity scopes

   Using the terms in Section 2.1, a platform P hosts tenants T with
   workloads W.  An account U may authorize several agents.  Section 2.3
   describes allocations at these scopes.  The noisy-neighbour boundary
   depends on which of these shares a budget.  A tenant budget separates
   tenants but leaves workloads within a tenant sharing its allocation.
   A workload budget separates workloads but still groups their Clients.
   Account budgets can isolate those allocations further, while several
   Clients sharing one account budget remain subject to the same limit.

   In Figure 4, Anchor W endorses workload-scoped allocations and Anchor
   U endorses account-scoped allocations.  Client components obtain and
   spend the corresponding Credentials.  The request carries both budget
   proofs alongside the platform's signature.  The Anchors need not
   operate the forwarding intermediary.  These are intended scopes,
   pending the multiple-presentation carriage in Section 4.4.

   +---------------------+       +---------------------+
   |      Anchor W       |       |      Anchor U       |
   +----------+----------+       +----------+----------+
              | Endorsement                 | Endorsement
              v                             v
   +--------------------------------------------------+
   | Platform P                                       |
   |  +--------------------------------------------+  |
   |  | Tenant workload with Clients                |  |
   |  +----------------------+---------------------+  |
   |                         |                        |
   |                         v                        |
   |                 +--------------+                 |
   |                 | Intermediary +-----------------+---+
   |                 +--------------+                 |   |

                                                    +-----------+

                             stacked deployment

   A principal can also receive several independent budgets, for example
   one per Client.  Authorization determines what each Client may do,
   while issuance policy constrains their combined allocation.  Adding
   Clients must not yield unbounded replacement budgets.  Aggregate
   platform limits still apply when individual budgets remain available.

   Concurrent requests need separate credentials.  Several Credentials
   provide separate balances, so assigning more Credentials can increase
   both concurrency and total consumption.  Shared-budget splitting and
   simultaneous checks of aggregate and individual anonymous budgets
   remain open.

   A CDN can operate a Moderator for several origins.  All servers
   accepting the same credential key and context must share atomic
   replay state.  Independent stores need disjoint keys or contexts.
   Sharing a credential context also shares its budget scope and can
   enlarge the anonymity set, but activity at one origin can then reduce
   the balance available at another.

   Anchors constrain new allocations, but the Moderator cannot identify
   the issuing Anchor from a redemption.  [MOLE-ARCH] leaves privacy-
   preserving Anchor feedback unresolved.  Removing an Anchor requires
   evidence from outside that anonymous redemption, and changing sets to
   probe Clients can reduce privacy.  Endorsements from several accepted
   Anchors can yield several budgets.

   Deployment also needs bounded verification work and replay storage.
   Invalid proofs can consume verification capacity, and replay markers
   must remain while their Credentials are accepted.  Aggregate controls
   remain necessary for availability, even though they can reintroduce
   shared fate.

8.  Security considerations

   The security goals and limitations of MOLE are described in
   [MOLE-ARCH], [MOLE-PROTOCOLS], and [EC-ACT].

8.1.  Request integrity

   An intermediary that sees a presentation can race the Client to spend
   it.  The request-bound profile of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS] must cover every
   component that affects authorization, including the body where
   relevant.  Single-use verification alone determines only which spend
   wins.  Binding does not prevent an intermediary from withholding or
   racing an identical request.

   A carrier signature should cover the MOLE presentation and the
   relevant request components.  It attributes that pairing to the
   carrier.  Protection against the carrier itself requires binding the
   Client's evidence to the operation.  This document does not define
   the intermediary signing profile.

   Sender-constrained access tokens limit reuse of stolen tokens, but
   DPoP does not protect the request body [DPOP].  Mutual TLS
   authenticates the connection at its TLS endpoint [MTLS], which may be
   an intermediary.  Application-layer signatures can preserve integrity
   across TLS termination [WIMSE-AIMS].  For signed HTTP content, the
   verifier checks Content-Digest against the received content and
   verifies a signature covering it, together with the other components
   needed to bind the operation [HTTPSIG] [DIGEST].

8.2.  Correlation and policy

   Unlinkable Credentials do not hide addresses, cookies, content, or
   timing.  A forwarding provider that knows the Client can disclose its
   requests to the origin.  This deployment does not prevent that
   collusion.

   Credential keys, contexts, and public amounts partition the anonymity
   set.  They should follow coarse policies shared by many Clients.
   Per-Client values or repeated balance probes can make presentations
   distinguishable.  The privacy requirements of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS] apply
   to allocation, spending, and refund policy.  A platform signature
   further narrows the observable set.

   A Moderator can end a credential chain without identifying its
   holder.  Clients therefore rely on it to apply the advertised policy.
   Withholding refunds can consume scarce Endorsements, while recovery
   distinguishes a lost response from a recorded termination only while
   the result remains available.
