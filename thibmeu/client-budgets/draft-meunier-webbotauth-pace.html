Proportionate Access and Capacity Enforcement

Abstract

Origins often limit requests by address or by the platform carrying them. Clients behind shared infrastructure then share the consequences of one another's activity. This document sketches independently allocated budgets that let origins bound consumption without requiring stable Client identifiers. It describes how MOLE credentials preserve budget continuity across unlinkable presentations, how carrier attribution supports aggregate limits, and where composition with resource authorization needs further work.¶

1. Introduction

Agents are increasingly used in business and user workflows, including AI assistants, search indexing, content aggregation, and automated testing. Their relationship with origins is symbiotic. They need information from origins, and origins need signals to apply rate-limit policies. These can be binary, like allow or block when an origin cannot serve traffic, or resource-based to ensure fair access to everyone.¶

Historically, most of these signals have been enshrined into deployments of internet protocols. An agent needs an IP address to receive a response from an origin. Its TLS stack and browser behavior provide further signals. It provides a familiar User-Agent because middleboxes might otherwise filter its traffic (see [USER-AGENT-HISTORY]).¶

Recent developments break these patterns. Agents use proxies ([CONNECT-UDP], [CONNECT-IP], [OHTTP], or plain [HTTP]). As platforms make requests on behalf of users, the user's TLS stack may no longer be the one visible to the origin. AI agents, on the user's device or hosted remotely, fetch and render information in place of browsers. User-Agent strings are still spoofed.¶

Origins need to apply policy despite these changes. Many approaches rely on obtaining a stable identifier. An address, including an [IPv6] address, is not necessarily a user. Blocking a shared IP range can affect everyone behind it. In enterprise settings, an agent can identify as a workload [WIMSE-AIMS]. Platforms that can tolerate attribution can identify using [WBA-HTTPSIG]. Other approaches involve user authentication or authorization, such as [AAUTH] and [OAUTH-XAA]. Section 4 places these options side by side.¶

While identification solves many problems, it should not be necessary just to limit an agent's consumption. The problem is to give origins that control without requiring users or their agents to disclose a stable identifier. This supports the user interests discussed in [INTERNET-FOR-END-USER].¶

1.1. Motivating use cases

In the cases below, traffic passes through platforms. Each user also has a relationship with some Anchor, often but not necessarily that platform, which can grant scarce Endorsements. Origins could use those to apply rate-limit policies without learning who the user is.¶

  1. AI agent services host remote agents and concentrate their traffic on a small set of IP addresses. One user's activity can affect others when an origin can only limit the shared address or the platform as a whole.¶

  2. Network operators carry traffic from device-resident agents behind shared IP ranges. Policies applied to an address range or an entire ASN give these users a shared fate.¶

  3. VPN providers and enterprise ZTNA gateways have a noisy neighbour problem. They may know their users, but should not have to expose them to each origin. An origin able to tell employees apart could single out an executive's activity for instance.¶

1.2. Related work

[WBA-ABA] shares the anonymous rate-limiting goal and the MOLE construction. This document is a companion to it. It focuses on platforms that already have a relationship with their users, and on composing with a platform's [WBA-HTTPSIG] identity.¶

Poppy [POPPY] uses User IDs and sessions, including for anonymous browsing. [AAP] uses anonymous admission tokens and recommends a session afterwards, though it also allows a fresh token per request. Here, the aim is to maintain a per-agent budget without linking its presentations.¶

[WIMSE-AIMS] profiles WIMSE and OAuth for agents. It treats an agent as a workload with its own identifier and credential, and carries the user, when there is one, only as the subject of an OAuth access token, so that the two identities are separate and compose on one request. [WAG] lets a platform-hosted workload obtain such a token for itself without per-workload registration. This document places both among the options an origin can ask for, next to attribution and anonymous capacity.¶

2. Conventions and Definitions

This document uses the HTTP terminology of [HTTP] and the MOLE terminology of [MOLE-ARCH]. The roles can be operated by separate parties or combined.¶

Client:

The MOLE participant that obtains an Endorsement, redeems it for a Credential, and presents that Credential when requesting a resource.¶

Anchor:

The party that grants Endorsements under a policy the Moderator accepts. Its relationship with a Client constrains access to new budgets.¶

Moderator:

The party that issues Credentials and verifies their presentations. It sets the budget policy and may be operated by the origin or on its behalf.¶

Origin:

The server that controls access to the requested resource.¶

Intermediary:

A proxy, gateway, or tunnel on the request path. An intermediary that terminates HTTP can sign forwarded requests under its own identity.¶

A Client may hold several budgets, and an account or workload may receive budgets for several Clients. Those allocations depend on Anchor and Moderator policy. A workload has the meaning given in [WIMSE-ARCH]. A Credential does not establish a unique account, workload, device, or other principal.¶

MOLE(C) denotes a presentation from Client C. WBA(P) denotes a Web Bot Auth signature from intermediary P. C is a label in the figures, not an identifier sent to the origin. A credential chain is the sequence of Credentials obtained by updating one initial Credential. The Client experiences continuity through that chain without giving the origin a session identifier.¶

3. Scope and principles

The aim is to bound consumption through independently allocated budgets without requiring a stable Client identifier. Exhausting one budget should not exhaust an unrelated budget. Enforcement still depends on constrained issuance and available capacity. A platform aggregate limit can affect every Client behind it even when their individual budgets remain usable.¶

For public resources, the deployment goal is a baseline level of access without a Credential or signature. This baseline remains subject to capacity and request behavior. Scarce Endorsements can support access above it. Absence of an active signal does not itself establish abuse [CIVIL]. Permissioned resources may require authorization before serving any content.¶

Clients choose which evidence to offer. Origins should ask only for evidence needed by the requested operation. A verified identity does not establish that a request is harmless, and a valid budget does not grant every permission. Access to accepted Anchors is uneven, so scarce issuance alone does not ensure fair allocation.¶

The privacy objective is to separate the relationship used to obtain a budget from the requests that spend it [PRIVACY-PARTITIONING]. Presentations should reveal only what the origin needs to apply its policy. Network metadata and other request fields can still identify or correlate Clients.¶

The flows below use the EC-ACT credential type of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS]. Other MOLE credential types can have different properties. This is a deployment sketch using work in progress. The composition gaps are listed in Section 4.4, and the underlying construction's security analysis remains incomplete [EC-ACT].¶

4. Attribution and authorization

An origin decides whether to serve an operation and how much capacity to allocate to it. The evidence it accepts can establish attribution, permission, or a remaining budget. A request may need more than one.¶

4.1. Attribution

A verified WBA(P) signature attributes its covered components to a key published by P [WBA-HTTPSIG]. The origin can apply an aggregate limit to P without receiving an identifier for each Client behind it. The signature does not establish permission unless origin policy grants it to that signer.¶

A workload can authenticate using a scheme such as [WIMSE-AIMS]. Its credential and signature profile differ from Web Bot Auth, even when both use HTTP Message Signatures [HTTPSIG]. A session identifier allows requests to be correlated within the session. Either form of attribution can accompany a budget, with the resulting loss of unlinkability within its scope.¶

An origin can request a signature with Accept-Signature. The Client may decline, and the origin may refuse access where its policy requires a signature. The public baseline in Section 3 is this deployment's goal, not a requirement imposed by Web Bot Auth.¶

4.2. Permission

Permission depends on the resource's policy. An origin can accept a capability without identifying its holder, including a Privacy Pass token when the issuer's attestation satisfies that policy [PRIVACYPASS] [PRIVATETOKEN]. A reused capability can still link requests.¶

An OAuth access token conveys an authorization grant [OAUTH]. The resource checks the token as its authorization server requires, including its audience, validity, permissions, and any proof of possession. Identity obtained through [OIDC] or workload authentication can also be an input to access policy. Authentication alone does not determine the permitted operations.¶

A workload can obtain a token using [WAG], and an existing federation can support delegated access through [OAUTH-XAA]. These grants are exchanged at the authorization server, which decides what access token to issue. They do not define a common token claim layout. [AAUTH] defines a separate signed authorization protocol with its own challenges and credentials.¶

4.3. Capacity

An EC-ACT Credential holds a hidden balance c. A presentation proves that it covers a requested spend s. The Moderator can return an amount t, bounded by s plus the offered top-up a. The replacement Credential holds c - s + t. The origin learns the public amounts and that the spend was possible, without learning the balance or a stable Client identifier [MOLE-PROTOCOLS].¶

For example, a policy can issue B credits, charge one per accepted presentation, and return zero with no top-ups. Each issuance then allows at most B fresh accepted operation attempts. Further issuances increase that allocation. Bounding consumption over time also requires policy for replenishment and expiry. Refunding every spend in full leaves successful consumption unbounded.¶

A principal that already authenticates may still need several independent budgets. Metering that principal does not replace those allocations. Conversely, adding MOLE to requests that already carry a stable identifier does not make those requests unlinkable.¶

4.4. Composition

A carrier signature and a MOLE presentation can travel on one request. The signature should cover the presentation and the relevant request components, as discussed in Section 7. Multiple signature profiles can use distinct labels, with each verifier applying the corresponding profile.¶

Permission and capacity can also be required together. However, MOLE, PrivateToken, and OAuth access tokens use the Authorization field, which carries one credentials value Section 11.6.2 of [HTTP]. The current MOLE transport also selects one presentation per request [MOLE-HTTP]. Carriage of several budgets or of a budget beside another authorization scheme remains undefined here.¶

WWW-Authenticate can carry several challenges, but that alone does not express that all must be satisfied. Signaling cumulative requirements is also open. The permissioned flow in Section 5.2 marks this boundary rather than specifying an encoding.¶

5. Moderating consumption

The origin configures its accepted Anchor Set, credential contexts, allocation and spending policy, and any permission requirements. A Client may send an initial request without evidence. The origin verifies what is present and serves the level of access its policy permits. A public response can offer more capacity through a MOLE challenge. A permissioned resource can refuse the operation and challenge for the missing authorization.¶

A MOLE redemption challenge lets a Client obtain a Credential. A presentation challenge specifies its spending terms. Clients use the exchanges and verification rules of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS] and [MOLE-HTTP], including replay checks and request binding. A failed proof supplies no evidence of budget.¶

For EC-ACT, the Moderator records the spend result atomically before attempting the operation. It chooses a refund or NoUpdate from the evidence available at that point. The response carries that recorded result even if the operation fails. Choosing a refund from the operation's eventual outcome would require additional settlement behavior not defined by this flow.¶

A refund replaces the spent Credential. A zero refund leaves c - s credits. NoUpdate ends the chain and forfeits its remaining balance. At the HTTP layer, an empty Mole-Credential update conveys NoUpdate. A missing update does not.¶

After a lost response, the Client can recover the recorded credential result within the retention period. Recovery does not repeat the protected operation or establish whether it completed. Application recovery remains separate. One credential chain permits one presentation in flight at a time.¶

5.1. Public access with WBA and MOLE

The origin offers a budget above its public baseline. Client C already holds an Endorsement from an accepted Anchor. Intermediary P adds its own signature. The Moderator is shown at the origin.¶

The origin verifies WBA(P) and applies any aggregate limit separately from the Client's budget. Because it serves the baseline, its optional MOLE challenges travel on a 200 response. A Client with a suitable Credential can skip redemption. Otherwise, it obtains the Moderator configuration and redeems its Endorsement at the Redeem & Issue endpoint.¶

On the budgeted request, the Client proves that its balance covers s and requests a top-up of at most a. The Moderator verifies and records the result before attempting the operation. The example continues the chain by returning an Update with 0 <= t <= s + a. Each subsequent spend answers an applicable presentation challenge. Successive fresh presentations have different nullifiers, and the Credential does not provide a link between them.¶

A direct Client uses the same exchange without WBA(P). A Client unable or unwilling to present continues at the public baseline, subject to origin policy. A failed presentation does not authorize the budgeted level of access.¶

5.2. Permissioned access with a separate budget

The Client requests an operation requiring an OAuth access token. The resource uses protected resource metadata [OAUTH-RS-META] to identify its authorization server. The grant issuer below is a Workload Identity Provider for WAG, or an Identity Provider Authorization Server for ID-JAG. Each requires an existing trust arrangement with the resource's authorization server.¶

For WAG, the Client exchanges a workload assertion for an access token. For ID-JAG, it obtains a grant through the existing federation and exchanges it at the resource's authorization server. The resource receives the resulting access token, not either grant. A Client with a suitable token skips this exchange. Bearer challenges follow [BEARER]. DPoP uses its own authentication scheme [DPOP]. Insufficient scope and authentication strength or freshness are distinct failures, handled by [BEARER] and [STEPUP], respectively.¶

The resource can meter an authenticated principal or require a separate budget for the Client's activity. Several Clients authorized under the same principal can have independent allocations. Requiring both permission and a MOLE budget needs the composition work described in Section 4.4. A workload signature alone does not replace the access token's permissions.¶

6. Deployment considerations

An endorsement relationship is independent of the traffic path. In Figure 3, the Anchor endorses Client C while intermediary P carries its requests. The same provider can operate both roles. A tunnel carrying end-to-end TLS cannot add an HTTP signature.¶

Independent allocations can coexist under one authenticated principal, as in Figure 4. Permission covers the operation each Client may request. Budget policy determines how much each may consume. Issuance policy must constrain the total allocation if creating another Client is cheap.¶

Concurrent requests need separate credential chains. Several Credentials provide separate balances, so assigning more Credentials can increase both concurrency and total consumption. Shared-budget splitting and simultaneous checks of aggregate and individual anonymous budgets remain open.¶

A CDN can operate a Moderator for several origins. All servers accepting the same credential key and context must share atomic replay state. Independent stores need disjoint keys or contexts. Sharing a credential context also shares its budget scope and can enlarge the anonymity set, but activity at one origin can then reduce the balance available at another.¶

Anchors constrain new allocations, but the Moderator cannot identify the issuing Anchor from a redemption. [MOLE-ARCH] leaves privacy-preserving Anchor feedback unresolved. Removing an Anchor requires evidence from outside that anonymous redemption, and changing sets to probe Clients can reduce privacy. Endorsements from several accepted Anchors can yield several budgets.¶

Deployment also needs bounded verification work and replay storage. Invalid proofs can consume verification capacity, and replay markers must remain while their Credentials are accepted. Aggregate controls remain necessary for availability, even though they can reintroduce shared fate.¶

7. Security considerations

The security goals and limitations of MOLE are described in [MOLE-ARCH], [MOLE-PROTOCOLS], and [EC-ACT].¶

7.1. Request integrity

An intermediary that sees a presentation can race the Client to spend it. The request-bound profile of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS] must cover every component that affects authorization, including the body where relevant. Single-use verification alone determines only which spend wins. Binding does not prevent an intermediary from withholding or racing an identical request.¶

A carrier signature should cover the MOLE presentation and the relevant request components. It attributes that pairing to the carrier. Protection against the carrier itself requires binding the Client's evidence to the operation. This document does not define the intermediary signing profile.¶

Sender-constrained access tokens limit reuse of stolen tokens, but DPoP does not protect the request body [DPOP]. Mutual TLS authenticates the connection at its TLS endpoint [MTLS], which may be an intermediary. Application-layer signatures can preserve integrity across TLS termination [WIMSE-AIMS]. For signed HTTP content, the verifier checks Content-Digest against the received content and verifies a signature covering it, together with the other components needed to bind the operation [HTTPSIG] [DIGEST].¶

7.2. Correlation and policy

Unlinkable Credentials do not hide addresses, cookies, content, or timing. A forwarding provider that knows the Client can disclose its requests to the origin. This deployment does not prevent that collusion.¶

Credential keys, contexts, and public amounts partition the anonymity set. They should follow coarse policies shared by many Clients. Per-Client values or repeated balance probes can make presentations distinguishable. The privacy requirements of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS] apply to allocation, spending, and refund policy. A platform signature further narrows the observable set.¶

A Moderator can end a credential chain without identifying its holder. Clients therefore rely on it to apply the advertised policy. Withholding refunds can consume scarce Endorsements, while recovery distinguishes a lost response from a recorded termination only while the result remains available.¶
