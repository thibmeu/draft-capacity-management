Anonymous Agent Authentication

Abstract

Origins often limit requests by address or by the platform carrying them. Clients behind shared infrastructure then share the consequences of one another's activity. Signed product, project, or session identifiers can narrow that shared fate, but also let origins link requests. When the goal is only to bound consumption, such identifiers need not be disclosed: this document sketches independently allocated, scarce budgets instead. It describes how MOLE credentials preserve budget continuity across unlinkable presentations, how carrier attribution supports aggregate limits, and where composition with resource authorization needs further work.¶

1. Introduction

Agents are increasingly used in business and user workflows, including AI assistants, search indexing, content aggregation, and automated testing. Their relationship with origins is symbiotic. They need information from origins, and origins need signals to apply rate-limit policies. These can be binary, like allow or block when an origin cannot serve traffic, or resource-based to ensure fair access to everyone.¶

Historically, most of these signals have been enshrined into deployments of internet protocols. An agent needs an IP address to receive a response from an origin. Its TLS stack and browser behavior provide further signals. It provides a familiar User-Agent because middleboxes might otherwise filter its traffic (see [USER-AGENT-HISTORY]).¶

Recent developments break these patterns. Agents use proxies ([CONNECT-UDP], [CONNECT-IP], [OHTTP], or plain [HTTP]). As platforms make requests on behalf of users, the user's TLS stack may no longer be the one visible to the origin. AI agents, on the user's device or hosted remotely, fetch and render information in place of browsers. User-Agent strings are still spoofed.¶

Origins need to apply policy despite these changes. An address, including an [IPv6] address, is not necessarily a user. Blocking a shared IP range can affect everyone behind it. A platform signing requests under one [WBA-HTTPSIG] identity creates a similar shared fate: when one tenant's agent overloads an origin, limiting the platform also limits its other tenants. [SIGNED-AGENT-METADATA] describes this problem and proposes signed product, project, and session identifiers to let an origin isolate traffic below the platform level.¶

More granular identifiers are useful when the origin needs to recognize a particular product or project, investigate abuse with its platform, or follow a task within a session. [SIGNED-AGENT-METADATA] advocates for such identifiers. While they are pseudonymous, the receiving origin can link requests within the context of such identifiers. This has two immediate costs:¶

  • Discriminatory treatment: Explicit identifiers allow for discriminatory treatment of traffic based on those identifiers. This could be used for the purposes of giving preferential treatment to some traffic over others, such as by blocking certain types of traffic depending on their requests.¶

  • Privacy: Explicit identifiers allow for linking traffic to that identifier. The more granular the identifier, the more precise of a profile this linkability offers.¶

This presents natural tension between the needs of an origin and the needs of an agent. Identifiers are sufficient for enforcing capacity and abuse control on the granularity of the identifiers provided, but identifiers can lead to harmful outcomes for agents. An origin needs to distinguish abusive from acceptable behavior, but does not always need a persistent handle on the party behind it.¶

If the origin's goal is only to bound each consumption to products, projects, or platforms, identifiers are not strictly necessary. This document describes Anonymous Agent Authentication (AAA) using MOLE (Moderation of unLinkable Endorsements), an architecture for private moderation [MOLE-ARCH]. Separately allocated budgets can keep one Client's spending from exhausting another's allocation behind shared infrastructure. This requires Anchor policies that constrain issuance and Moderator policies that charge and replenish balances; MOLE alone does not ensure one budget per agent or enforce a per-agent rate over time. Public access need not be conditioned on disclosure of a stable identifier.¶

Such a mechanism is useful in a number of cases. Some examples follow:¶

  1. An AI agent service that hosts remote agents and concentrate their traffic on a small set of IP addresses. One user's activity can affect others when an origin can only limit the shared address or the platform as a whole.¶

  2. Network operators carry traffic from device-resident agents behind shared IP ranges. Policies applied to an address range or an entire ASN give these users a shared fate.¶

  3. VPN providers and enterprise zero trust gateways have a noisy neighbour problem. They may know their users, but should not have to expose them to each origin. An origin able to tell employees apart could single out an executive's activity for instance.¶

1.1. Related work

[SIGNED-AGENT-METADATA] addresses shared fate under a platform's Web Bot Auth signature with signed, pseudonymous sub-identifiers. It lets an origin apply policy to a product, project, or session while the platform can map those identifiers back to its customers. Origin-scoped session values reduce cross-origin correlation (Section 7.2 of [SIGNED-AGENT-METADATA]), but each identifier still links requests within its own scope and lifetime. MOLE serves the case where the origin needs a scarce budget without such a handle; it does not provide the same tenant-specific abuse reporting or targeted blocking of an identified project.¶

[WBA-ABA] also uses MOLE for anonymous bot access and rate limiting. This document focuses on the shared-fate problem beneath a platform's [WBA-HTTPSIG] identity: scarce budgets can isolate consumption without asking the platform to expose product, project, or session identifiers for that purpose. It does not replace signed metadata where the origin needs to attribute activity to one of those units.¶

Poppy [POPPY] uses User IDs and sessions, including for anonymous browsing. [AAP] uses anonymous admission tokens and recommends a session afterwards, though it also allows a fresh token per request. Here, the aim is to maintain a scarce, independently allocated budget without linking its presentations.¶

[WIMSE-AIMS] profiles WIMSE and OAuth for agents. It treats an agent as a workload with its own identifier and credential, and carries the user, when there is one, only as the subject of an OAuth access token, so that the two identities are separate and compose on one request. [WAG] lets a platform-hosted workload obtain such a token for itself without per-workload registration. This document places both among the options an origin can ask for, next to attribution and anonymous capacity.¶

2. Conventions and Definitions

This document uses the HTTP terminology of [HTTP] and the MOLE terminology of [MOLE-ARCH]. The roles can be operated by separate parties or combined.¶

Client:

The MOLE participant that obtains an Endorsement, redeems it for a Credential, and presents that Credential when requesting a resource.¶

Anchor:

The party that grants Endorsements under a policy the Moderator accepts. Its relationship with a Client constrains access to new budgets.¶

Moderator:

The party that issues Credentials and verifies their presentations. It sets the budget policy and may be operated by the origin or on its behalf.¶

Origin:

The server that controls access to the requested resource.¶

Intermediary:

A proxy, gateway, or tunnel on the request path. An intermediary that terminates HTTP can sign forwarded requests under its own identity.¶

A Client may hold several budgets, and an account or workload may receive budgets for several Clients. Those allocations depend on Anchor and Moderator policy. A workload has the meaning given in [WIMSE-ARCH]. A Credential does not establish a unique account, workload, device, or other principal.¶

MOLE(X) denotes a presentation for budget scope X. MOLE(C) is a Client's budget, while MOLE(T), MOLE(W), and MOLE(U) denote tenant, workload, and account scopes. These labels do not appear as identifiers in presentations. WBA(Y) denotes a Web Bot Auth signature from intermediary Y.¶

3. Scope and principles

The aim of AAA is to bound consumption through independently allocated budgets without requiring a stable client identifier. Exhausting one budget should not exhaust another allocation, provided Anchor issuance and Moderator spending policies keep them separate. The privacy objective is to separate the relationship used to obtain a budget from the requests that spend it [PRIVACY-PARTITIONING]. A platform aggregate limit can still affect every Client behind it even when their individual budgets remain usable.¶

For public resources, the deployment goal is a baseline level of access without a credential or signature. This baseline remains subject to capacity and request behavior. Scarce Endorsements can support access above it. Absence of an active signal does not itself establish abuse [CIVIL].¶

Permissioned resources may require evidence of a right before serving any content. A capability can establish that right without identifying its holder. Where a resource's policy depends on a particular principal and its permissions, authenticating the principal with a stable identifier is appropriate. Capacity evidence alone establishes neither identity nor a right to perform a permissioned operation.¶

Clients choose which evidence to offer. Origins should ask only for evidence needed by the requested operation. A verified identity does not establish that a request is harmless, and a valid budget does not grant every permission. Moreover, client evidence should reveal only what the origin needs to apply its policy. Network metadata and other request fields can still identify or correlate clients.¶

4. Attribution and authorization

An origin decides whether to serve an operation and how much capacity to allocate to it. The evidence it accepts can establish attribution, permission, or capacity. A request may need more than one.¶

4.1. Attribution

A verified WBA(P) signature attributes its covered components to a key published by P [WBA-HTTPSIG]. The origin can apply an aggregate limit to P without receiving an identifier for each Client behind it. The signature does not establish permission unless origin policy grants it to that signer.¶

A workload can authenticate using a scheme such as [WIMSE-AIMS]. Its credential and signature profile differ from Web Bot Auth, even when both use HTTP Message Signatures [HTTPSIG]. A session identifier allows requests to be correlated within the session. Either form of attribution can accompany a budget, with the resulting loss of unlinkability within its scope.¶

An origin can request a signature with Accept-Signature. The Client may decline, and the origin may refuse access where its policy requires a signature. The public baseline in Section 3 is this deployment's goal, not a requirement imposed by Web Bot Auth.¶

4.2. Permission

Permission depends on the resource's policy. An origin can accept a capability without identifying its holder, including a Privacy Pass token when the issuer's attestation satisfies that policy [PRIVACYPASS] [PRIVATETOKEN]. A reused capability can still link requests.¶

An OAuth access token conveys an authorization grant [OAUTH]. The resource checks the token as its authorization server requires, including its audience, validity, permissions, and any proof of possession. Identity obtained through [OIDC] or workload authentication can also be an input to access policy. Authentication alone does not determine the permitted operations.¶

A workload can obtain a token using [WAG], and an existing federation can support delegated access through [OAUTH-XAA]. These grants are exchanged at the authorization server, which decides what access token to issue. They do not define a common token claim layout. [AAUTH] defines a separate signed authorization protocol with its own challenges and credentials.¶

4.3. Capacity

MOLE credentials hold a hidden balance c. A presentation proves that it covers a requested spend s. The moderator can return an amount t, bounded by s plus the offered top-up a. The replacement credential holds c - s + t. The origin learns the public amounts and that the spend was possible, without learning the balance or a stable client identifier [MOLE-PROTOCOLS].¶

For example, a policy can issue B credits, charge one per accepted presentation, and return zero with no top-ups. Each issuance then allows at most B fresh accepted operation attempts. Further issuances increase that allocation. Bounding consumption over time also requires policy for replenishment and expiry. Refunding every spend in full leaves successful consumption unbounded.¶

4.4. Composition

A carrier signature and a MOLE presentation can travel on one request. The signature should cover the presentation and the relevant request components, as discussed in Section 7. Multiple signature profiles can use distinct labels, with each verifier applying the corresponding profile.¶

Permission and capacity can also be required together. However, MOLE, PrivateToken, and OAuth access tokens use the Authorization field, which carries one credentials value Section 11.6.2 of [HTTP]. The current MOLE transport also selects one presentation per request [MOLE-HTTP]. Carriage of several budgets or of a budget beside another authorization scheme remains undefined here.¶

WWW-Authenticate can carry several challenges, but that alone does not express that all must be satisfied. Signaling cumulative requirements is also open; this document does not specify the permissioned flow or its encoding.¶

5. Moderating consumption

The origin's moderator configures its accepted Anchor Set, credential contexts, allocation and spending policy, and any permission requirements. A client may send an initial request without evidence. The origin verifies what is present and serves the level of access its policy permits. A public response can offer more capacity through a MOLE challenge. A permissioned resource can refuse the operation and challenge for the missing authorization.¶

A MOLE redemption challenge lets a client obtain a credential. A presentation challenge specifies its spending terms. Clients use the exchanges and verification rules of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS] and [MOLE-HTTP], including replay checks and request binding. A failed proof supplies no evidence of budget. A refund replaces the spent Credential with an updated one.¶

After a lost response, the Client can recover the recorded credential result within the retention period. Recovery does not repeat the protected operation or establish whether it completed. Application recovery remains separate. One credential permits one presentation in flight at a time.¶

5.1. Public access with WBA and MOLE

The origin offers a budget above its public baseline. Client C already holds an Endorsement from an accepted Anchor. Intermediary P adds its own signature. The Moderator is shown at the origin.¶

  1. The Client sends a request. The intermediary adds WBA(P), which the origin verifies before applying its aggregate limit.¶

  2. The origin serves the public baseline and offers redemption and presentation challenges on the 200 response. A Client with a suitable Credential skips redemption. Otherwise, it fetches the Moderator configuration and redeems its Endorsement at the Redeem & Issue endpoint.¶

  3. The Client presents proof that its balance covers s and requests a top-up of at most a. The Moderator verifies and records the spend result atomically before attempting the operation.¶

  4. The response returns an Update with 0 <= t <= s + a, continuing the chain with balance c - s + t. Each subsequent spend answers an applicable challenge and uses a fresh nullifier, without a credential link to the previous presentation.¶

A direct Client uses the same exchange without WBA(P). A Client unable or unwilling to present continues at the public baseline, subject to origin policy. A failed presentation does not authorize the budgeted level of access.¶

6. Deployment considerations

Deployment separates two kinds of capacity control: an intermediary can identify itself for an aggregate limit, while an Anchor allocates scarce Endorsements for smaller, anonymous budgets. Figure 2 shows the Anchor endorsing Client C while intermediary P carries its requests. The same provider can operate both roles, but need not. A tunnel carrying end-to-end TLS cannot add an HTTP signature.¶

The origin can attribute the forwarded request to P through WBA(P) and charge C's separate budget through MOLE(C), without learning C's identity from that presentation. The allocation relationship, rather than P's signature, establishes which smaller unit bears the spend. The following subsection applies this separation to product, project, and session capacity limits.¶

6.1. Capacity without identifiers

Signed product, project, and session IDs give an origin visible keys with which to keep separate counters [SIGNED-AGENT-METADATA]. For capacity alone, the Anchor can constrain Endorsements per product, project, or task allocation only if it knows and enforces those boundaries. The platform can play this role; an independent Anchor needs a way to account for the same allocations, or its Endorsements cannot enforce the platform's project and product totals.¶

Clients redeem those endorsements for independent MOLE credentials and spend the credentials on requests. The Moderator checks each spend and carries the remaining balance into an unlinkable replacement Credential. The presentation itself does not reveal which product, project, task, or Client holds a particular budget.¶

The platform can still sign WBA(P), so the origin can also limit its traffic in aggregate. Credential contexts and spending terms should be shared across many endorsements; unique contexts or amounts would distinguish those units and shrink their anonymity sets.¶

The allocation boundary can be chosen without adding a corresponding ID to requests:¶

  • Product: A platform or other suitable Anchor can reserve a finite pool of Endorsements for a product and distribute them among its projects or Clients. Spending is limited by the budgets derived from that pool. The origin does not learn which product a presentation came from, nor can it impose a different per-product policy from presentations alone.¶

  • Project: The platform can cap Endorsements for a tenant workspace and allocate them among its Clients. Exhausting one Client's independent Credential does not exhaust another's. The platform, not the origin, accounts for how many endorsements belong to that project. A single project-wide balance shared by many concurrent Clients is a different requirement, discussed below.¶

  • Session: An Anchor can allocate Endorsements to a task (like an ephemeral workload). The Anchor must count task allocations against a scarce parent allowance so starting new tasks does not reset the limit. The allocating party and Client must keep the resulting Credentials assigned to those tasks: a MOLE presentation does not prove which task is using a Credential. Alternatively, a Client can spend the same Credential across tasks when task-specific budgets are unnecessary. Neither choice lets the origin link requests as one task; that requires a session identifier if the origin needs it.¶

For example, suppose an Anchor grants at most two Endorsements per project per epoch and the Moderator issues each Credential with ten credits. If each accepted request spends one credit, with no refund or top-up, and the Credentials expire at the epoch boundary, that project's endorsements permit at most twenty budgeted requests in the epoch. The origin verifies the ten-credit limit on each Credential; the twenty-request total holds only if the Anchor actually issues no more than two Endorsements for that project and no other accepted Anchor supplies additional ones.¶

The Anchor can also cap the sum of Endorsements across projects belonging to one product. The origin enforces each Credential's ten-credit limit without seeing the project or product; the project and product totals depend on the Anchor's issuance policy, not on an origin-side counter keyed by an ID. Additional accepted Anchors or Endorsements increase the available budget unless their policies constrain the combined allocation. Public-baseline requests and the platform's aggregate limit remain separate from this bound.¶

These budgets replace identifiers only for the capacity decisions their issuance and spending policies implement. An origin cannot use an anonymous presentation to identify and block a particular project, audit its activity, or correlate all requests in a session. If it needs those functions, signed metadata can accompany MOLE, making requests linkable at the metadata's scope.¶

6.2. Capacity scopes

A platform P can host tenants T, each with workloads W serving several Clients. An account U may authorize several Clients. The noisy-neighbour boundary depends on which of these shares a budget. A tenant budget separates tenants but leaves workloads within a tenant sharing its allocation. A workload budget separates workloads but still groups their Clients. Account budgets can isolate those allocations further, while several Clients sharing one account budget remain subject to the same limit.¶

In Figure 3, Anchor W endorses a workload and Anchor U endorses account allocations. The workload and its Clients play separate MOLE roles. The request carries their budget proofs alongside the platform's signature. The Anchors need not operate the forwarding intermediary. These are intended scopes, pending the multiple-presentation carriage in Section 4.4.¶

A principal can also receive several independent budgets, for example one per Client. Authorization determines what each Client may do, while issuance policy constrains their combined allocation. Adding Clients must not yield unbounded replacement budgets. Aggregate platform limits still apply when individual budgets remain available.¶

Concurrent requests need separate credentials. Several Credentials provide separate balances, so assigning more Credentials can increase both concurrency and total consumption. Shared-budget splitting and simultaneous checks of aggregate and individual anonymous budgets remain open.¶

A CDN can operate a Moderator for several origins. All servers accepting the same credential key and context must share atomic replay state. Independent stores need disjoint keys or contexts. Sharing a credential context also shares its budget scope and can enlarge the anonymity set, but activity at one origin can then reduce the balance available at another.¶

Anchors constrain new allocations, but the Moderator cannot identify the issuing Anchor from a redemption. [MOLE-ARCH] leaves privacy-preserving Anchor feedback unresolved. Removing an Anchor requires evidence from outside that anonymous redemption, and changing sets to probe Clients can reduce privacy. Endorsements from several accepted Anchors can yield several budgets.¶

Deployment also needs bounded verification work and replay storage. Invalid proofs can consume verification capacity, and replay markers must remain while their Credentials are accepted. Aggregate controls remain necessary for availability, even though they can reintroduce shared fate.¶

7. Security considerations

The security goals and limitations of MOLE are described in [MOLE-ARCH], [MOLE-PROTOCOLS], and [EC-ACT].¶

7.1. Request integrity

An intermediary that sees a presentation can race the Client to spend it. The request-bound profile of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS] must cover every component that affects authorization, including the body where relevant. Single-use verification alone determines only which spend wins. Binding does not prevent an intermediary from withholding or racing an identical request.¶

A carrier signature should cover the MOLE presentation and the relevant request components. It attributes that pairing to the carrier. Protection against the carrier itself requires binding the Client's evidence to the operation. This document does not define the intermediary signing profile.¶

Sender-constrained access tokens limit reuse of stolen tokens, but DPoP does not protect the request body [DPOP]. Mutual TLS authenticates the connection at its TLS endpoint [MTLS], which may be an intermediary. Application-layer signatures can preserve integrity across TLS termination [WIMSE-AIMS]. For signed HTTP content, the verifier checks Content-Digest against the received content and verifies a signature covering it, together with the other components needed to bind the operation [HTTPSIG] [DIGEST].¶

7.2. Correlation and policy

Unlinkable Credentials do not hide addresses, cookies, content, or timing. A forwarding provider that knows the Client can disclose its requests to the origin. This deployment does not prevent that collusion.¶

Credential keys, contexts, and public amounts partition the anonymity set. They should follow coarse policies shared by many Clients. Per-Client values or repeated balance probes can make presentations distinguishable. The privacy requirements of [MOLE-PROTOCOLS] apply to allocation, spending, and refund policy. A platform signature further narrows the observable set.¶

A Moderator can end a credential chain without identifying its holder. Clients therefore rely on it to apply the advertised policy. Withholding refunds can consume scarce Endorsements, while recovery distinguishes a lost response from a recorded termination only while the result remains available.¶
